Exit Strategies: Sell, Lease, or Reinvest?

Oliver Thornton · April 9, 2025

General information only. This article is educational and is not legal, tax, financial, lending or investment advice. Do your own due diligence and confirm everything with your own qualified professionals before relying on it. Read the full disclaimer.

How a project exits determines what an investor actually receives. The acquisition sets the ceiling — you cannot sell your way out of buying badly — but the exit is where a paper return becomes a real one, and where a good project can still disappoint.

There are three broad routes out of a residential development or repositioning: sell the asset, refinance and hold it, or hold and lease it as it stands. This covers what each involves, when each fits, and why the decision is made long before the project is finished.

The Exit Is Chosen at Acquisition, Not at Completion

The most common misconception is that the exit is a decision made once the work is done. It is not. The exit assumption drives the underwriting: what you can pay for the property, how much debt you can carry, what the debt is structured to do, and how long the capital is committed.

A project underwritten to sell and a project underwritten to hold are different projects on the same parcel. Different loan, different reserve, different finish specification. Deciding late means discovering that the structure you built does not support the exit you now want.

Option One: Sell

The most straightforward route. The asset is listed, sold, the loan is repaid, and proceeds are distributed through the waterfall.

When it fits: completed redevelopments and ground-up projects where the finished product is worth materially more than the cost to create it, and where the buyer pool is deep — which in Los Angeles residential generally means owner-occupant demand rather than investor demand.

What it costs: selling is not free. Brokerage, closing costs and transfer taxes come off the top, and in the City of Los Angeles the transfer tax on higher-value sales is substantial enough to belong in the underwriting rather than as a footnote. Timing risk is real too — a project completing into a soft quarter has no way to wait unless the debt allows it.

Option Two: Refinance and Hold

Rather than selling, the sponsor replaces the construction or bridge loan with longer-term debt once the property is stabilised. That refinancing can return some or all of investor capital while the asset continues to be owned.

When it fits: assets that produce durable income once complete — typically multifamily rather than single-family — where the stabilised value supports enough new debt to repay the original loan and return meaningful equity.

What it depends on: two things outside the sponsor's control. Interest rates at the moment of refinancing, and the appraised value at that moment. A plan that works at one rate can fail at another, which is why a refinance-dependent business plan should always be underwritten with a sale as the fallback.

Option Three: Hold and Lease

Complete the work, lease the property, and hold it for income and long-term appreciation without refinancing out.

When it fits: when the market for sales is genuinely poor and the asset produces enough income to service its debt comfortably while waiting. Holding is a legitimate strategy and it is also, sometimes, what a sponsor does when they cannot execute the intended exit. The difference matters, and the honest way to tell them apart is whether the hold was underwritten from the start.

What it means for investors: capital stays committed. Distributions may begin, but the return of principal does not arrive until a later sale or refinance. An investor who needed liquidity on the original timeline does not get it.

The Unit-by-Unit Exit

A fourth route exists for small multifamily: sell the building as individual homes rather than as a whole. Because the aggregate value of individual units generally exceeds the value of the building sold to an income buyer at a cap rate, the spread is real — and where condominium mapping is unavailable, tenancy in common is the structure that reaches it.

The tradeoff is time. Each unit waits on its own buyer arranging their own financing, so a sellout runs months rather than weeks, and the debt has to be structured with partial-release terms that permit it.

Why Two Exits Beat One

The strongest underwriting does not pick an exit; it establishes that more than one works.

A project that only performs if it sells at a specific price in a specific quarter is a project with a single point of failure. One that can sell, or refinance and hold, or fall back to a bulk sale at a lower but acceptable outcome, has room to absorb a market that does not cooperate. Ask a sponsor what the second exit is. If there isn't a clear answer, the plan depends on conditions rather than on execution.

Tax Treatment Is Not a Footnote

Exit route affects tax outcome substantially — holding period and the character of the gain, depreciation recapture on assets that were held and depreciated, and whether an exchange into a replacement property is available and appropriate. These interact with each investor's own position, and they are genuinely complex. They belong in a conversation with your own tax advisor before committing, not after a sale closes.

What to Ask Before Investing

  • What is the primary exit, and what is the fallback if it is unavailable?
  • Does the business plan depend on refinancing? At what rate and value does it stop working?
  • What are the transaction costs at exit, and are they in the model?
  • What happens if the project completes into a weak market — does the debt permit waiting?
  • Who decides when to sell, and does that decision require investor consent?

That last question is worth asking explicitly. In most syndications the timing of the exit sits with the general partner, not with investors.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is an exit strategy in real estate investing?
The planned route by which a project converts back into cash for its investors — most commonly selling the asset, refinancing it into long-term debt while continuing to hold, or holding and leasing it. The exit assumption drives the underwriting, so it is set at acquisition rather than chosen at completion.

Is it better to sell or refinance?
Neither is better in the abstract. Selling returns capital and ends the risk; refinancing can return capital while retaining an asset that produces income and may appreciate, but depends on interest rates and appraised value at that future moment. Refinance-dependent plans should carry a sale fallback.

What happens if a sponsor cannot sell?
The usual outcomes are holding longer than planned, leasing the asset in the interim, or selling at a lower price. All three affect investor returns, and the ability to wait depends on whether the debt permits it. This is why the downside case matters more than the projection.

Who decides when to exit a syndication?
Generally the sponsor. Operating agreements typically reserve only a narrow set of decisions for investor consent, and routine timing of a sale is usually not among them. The reserved-matters clause of the operating agreement defines the exact extent of investor say.

How long does a real estate project take to exit?
It varies by strategy. A renovation may run twelve to eighteen months; a ground-up development with an entitlement phase can run several years; a unit-by-unit sellout adds an absorption period on top of construction. Every timeline is an estimate, and business plans run long more often than short.
