Risk vs. Return: Questions to Ask Before You Invest

Oliver Thornton · April 30, 2025

A projected return is the least reliable number in an offering document, because it is the one the sponsor chose. Every other figure describes something that exists; the projection describes something that has not happened yet, under assumptions selected by the party asking for your money.

That does not make projections useless. It makes them a starting point for questions rather than an answer. What follows are the questions worth asking, what a good answer sounds like, and what a weak one is trying to avoid.

Risk and Return Are the Same Question

A higher projected return is not a better deal. It is a statement about how much uncertainty sits between here and there — and sometimes about how optimistic the assumptions are.

Two projects can project identical returns and carry entirely different risk: one leveraged to 80% with an eighteen-month entitlement still outstanding, the other at 55% on a fully permitted site. The projection does not distinguish them. The underwriting does.

What are the underwriting assumptions?

Every projection rests on a small number of load-bearing assumptions. Ask which ones, and ask what happens when each is wrong.

  • Exit price or exit cap rate. Based on what — comparable sales that have closed, or an assumption that the market improves?
  • Construction cost. Bid, estimated, or benchmarked from the sponsor's own recent projects on comparable work?
  • Timeline. How long from close to completion, and what does the model assume about entitlement?
  • Rent or absorption, where the plan depends on either.

A sponsor who can immediately tell you which assumption the deal is most sensitive to has done the work. One who describes every assumption as conservative has usually not tested any of them.

What does the downside case actually look like?

This is the single most useful question in the list, and the one most often answered vaguely.

You are not asking for a worse-looking version of the same model. You are asking: at what exit price does this project return capital but no profit? At what price does it lose money? How much would costs have to overrun, or the timeline slip, before the outcome changes materially?

Those are specific numbers and a sponsor who underwrites properly will have them. Downside protection is not a marketing phrase — it is whether the structure survives conditions that are worse than planned.

How much leverage, and on what terms?

Debt amplifies outcomes in both directions, and the terms matter as much as the amount.

Worth establishing: loan-to-cost or loan-to-value, whether the rate is fixed or floating, when the loan matures relative to the expected exit, and what happens if the project is not finished or sold by then. A loan maturing before a realistic exit forces decisions nobody wants to make — refinancing into a market you did not choose, or selling on someone else's schedule.

Ask whether extension options exist and what triggers them.

How is construction risk controlled?

In development, cost and time overruns are the most common way returns erode — more common than the market moving against you.

The useful questions are structural. Is there a contingency, and how large as a percentage of hard costs? Are subcontractor prices bid or estimated? Does the sponsor carry construction in-house or subcontract the whole thing — and if the latter, what happens when the general contractor is the one who is wrong? Vertical integration changes who absorbs that risk.

What is the sponsor's actual track record?

Completed projects, not projects in progress. Across a full market cycle, not only during a rising one. Any strategy looks capable in an appreciating market; what distinguishes operators is what happened to the deals that ran into trouble.

Ask directly about a project that went badly and what they did about it. A sponsor with a real history has one. A sponsor who claims never to have had a difficult deal is either new or not being straight with you. Evaluating the sponsor matters more than evaluating the property, because you are buying their execution of a plan, not the building.

Where does the sponsor's money sit?

What the sponsor has at stake and on what terms relative to you. That can be capital in the deal, personal guarantees on the debt, or fees deferred behind your return. If the sponsor has capital in, check whether it sits alongside yours or ahead of it in the waterfall.

Then the fee load: acquisition, asset management, construction management and disposition fees in combination. Fees are legitimate — running a project costs money — but if the total is large enough, a sponsor can be paid well on a deal that treats its investors poorly. The balance between fees and promote tells you whether they are aligned with the outcome or with the transaction.

What is the liquidity reality?

Private real estate positions generally cannot be sold when you want out. Establish the expected hold, what happens if it runs long, whether there is any transfer mechanism at all, and whether the sponsor can call additional capital — and what happens to an investor who does not fund it.

Money you might need is not money for this, and no answer to any other question changes that.

What a Weak Answer Sounds Like

  • Describing every assumption as conservative without saying what was tested.
  • Answering a downside question with a range rather than a number.
  • Track record given as total dollars transacted rather than projects completed and their outcomes.
  • Fees described as market-standard instead of stated.
  • Urgency — a closing date used as a reason not to finish diligence.

None of these is proof of anything. All of them are worth slowing down for. The document-level red flags cover what to look for in the paperwork itself.

Frequently Asked Questions

How do you evaluate risk in a real estate syndication?
By testing the assumptions rather than the projection: what exit price, cost and timeline the model requires, what happens when each is wrong, how much leverage is in place and when it matures, and whether the sponsor has completed comparable projects through a full market cycle.

What is a good return for a real estate investment?
There is no threshold that is good independent of risk. A higher projected return generally reflects more uncertainty, more leverage, or more optimistic assumptions. The useful comparison is between deals of similar risk, not between projections.

What questions should I ask a real estate sponsor?
What the deal is most sensitive to; what the downside case is in specific numbers; how much leverage and when it matures; how construction risk is controlled; what they have completed and what went wrong; what they have at stake, from capital invested to personal guarantees on the debt; the total fee load; and the expected hold with what happens if it runs long.

Are projected returns reliable?
They are projections, not obligations, and they are produced by the party raising capital. They are useful for understanding what a sponsor is assuming, and unreliable as predictions. The structure of the deal and the quality of the underwriting are more informative than the headline number.

What is the biggest risk in private real estate?
In development, execution — cost and timeline overruns are more common than adverse market moves. Across all strategies the concentrated risk is the sponsor, because the same asset in different hands produces different outcomes.
