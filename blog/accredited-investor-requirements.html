Accredited Investor Requirements: What the SEC Actually Requires

Oliver Thornton · June 3, 2025

General information only. This article is educational and is not legal, tax, financial, lending or investment advice. Do your own due diligence and confirm everything with your own qualified professionals before relying on it. Read the full disclaimer.

Most private real estate offerings — syndications, funds, private placements — are open only to accredited investors. It is a status defined by the Securities and Exchange Commission under Regulation D, not a marketing term and not something a sponsor can grant you.

The thresholds are specific, there are more routes to qualifying than most people realise, and how the status gets checked differs sharply depending on which exemption an offering uses. This guide covers each.

What is an accredited investor?

An individual or entity meeting defined financial or professional criteria that the SEC treats as evidence of the sophistication, or the financial cushion, to bear the risk of an investment that is less regulated, less liquid and less transparent than a public security.

The logic is disclosure. Public offerings carry extensive mandatory disclosure; private ones do not. Accreditation is the line the SEC draws to decide who may invest without that protection.

The Income Test

An individual qualifies with earned income exceeding $200,000 in each of the two most recent years, or $300,000 jointly with a spouse or spousal equivalent, together with a reasonable expectation of reaching the same level in the current year.

Two details that catch people out. The income must have been achieved in each of the two prior years, not averaged across them. And if you qualify jointly, the expectation of continuing at that level applies jointly too.

The Net Worth Test

An individual qualifies with a net worth exceeding $1,000,000, alone or jointly with a spouse, excluding the value of their primary residence.

The primary-residence exclusion carries two anti-abuse rules worth understanding:

  • Debt secured by the primary residence in excess of the home's value counts as a liability against net worth.
  • Any increase in mortgage debt on the primary residence in the 60 days before investing also counts as a liability — preventing someone from borrowing against their home to manufacture qualifying net worth.

Professional Certifications

Since a 2020 amendment, accreditation is no longer purely financial. Individuals holding certain professional credentials in good standing qualify regardless of income or net worth — currently the Series 7, Series 65 and Series 82 licences.

This was a meaningful change. It acknowledged that demonstrated financial knowledge is a better proxy for the ability to evaluate a private offering than wealth alone. The Series 65 in particular is obtainable by examination without sponsorship by a broker-dealer.

Two further routes exist: knowledgeable employees of a private fund qualify with respect to that fund, and family clients of a qualifying family office may qualify through it.

Entities

Entities qualify by several paths — most commonly assets exceeding $5,000,000 where the entity was not formed specifically to acquire the securities offered, or an entity in which all equity owners are themselves accredited investors. Certain institutions — banks, registered investment companies, insurers, and specified employee benefit plans — qualify by category. Trusts generally qualify with over $5,000,000 in assets and a sophisticated person directing the investment.

How the Status Actually Gets Checked

This is the part most explanations omit, and it is the difference you will experience in practice. It depends entirely on which exemption the offering relies on.

Under Rule 506(b), the sponsor may not advertise the offering publicly. Investors generally self-certify their status — typically a questionnaire in the subscription agreement — and the sponsor may rely on that representation absent reason to doubt it. This is why so many private deals reach people only through existing relationships: the sponsor cannot solicit strangers.

Under Rule 506(c), the sponsor may market publicly, but must take reasonable steps to verify that every investor is accredited. A checkbox is not sufficient. Verification usually means providing tax returns or W-2s, brokerage and bank statements together with a credit report, or — most commonly in practice — a written confirmation from your CPA, attorney, registered investment adviser or broker-dealer.

If you are asked for documentation you did not expect, that is generally a 506(c) offering doing what the rule requires, not a sponsor being intrusive.

What Accreditation Does Not Mean

Three things worth being clear about, because the status is easy to over-read.

It is not a quality signal. That an offering is limited to accredited investors says nothing whatsoever about whether it is a good investment. It describes who may participate, not what they are participating in.

It is not protection. Accredited investors can and do lose their entire investment. The status means the SEC has decided you may proceed without the disclosure regime that governs public offerings — which is less protection, not more.

It is not permanent. Accreditation is assessed at the time of each investment. Income and net worth change, and a status that qualified you two years ago may not qualify you now.

Non-Accredited and Sophisticated Investors

Rule 506(b) permits up to 35 non-accredited but sophisticated investors in an offering — those with sufficient knowledge and experience to evaluate its merits and risks. In practice many sponsors decline to include them, because doing so triggers substantially heavier disclosure requirements. A 506(c) offering may not include them at all.

Frequently Asked Questions

What are the accredited investor requirements?
An individual qualifies with income over $200,000 in each of the two most recent years ($300,000 jointly with a spouse) and a reasonable expectation of the same in the current year; or a net worth over $1,000,000 excluding their primary residence; or by holding a Series 7, Series 65 or Series 82 licence in good standing.

Does my house count toward accredited investor net worth?
No. The value of a primary residence is excluded. Mortgage debt above the home's value counts against you, as does any increase in that mortgage in the 60 days before investing.

How do you prove you are an accredited investor?
It depends on the offering. Under Rule 506(b) investors generally self-certify through a questionnaire. Under Rule 506(c) the sponsor must take reasonable steps to verify — commonly tax returns, brokerage and bank statements with a credit report, or a written confirmation from your CPA, attorney or investment adviser.

Can you become an accredited investor without being wealthy?
Yes. Holding a Series 7, Series 65 or Series 82 licence in good standing qualifies an individual regardless of income or net worth. The Series 65 can be obtained by examination without broker-dealer sponsorship.

Can non-accredited investors invest in a syndication?
Sometimes. A Rule 506(b) offering may include up to 35 non-accredited but sophisticated investors, though many sponsors decline because it triggers heavier disclosure obligations. A 506(c) offering cannot include them.

Does accredited status make an investment safer?
No. It determines who may invest, not whether the investment is sound. Accredited investors participate in offerings that carry less mandatory disclosure than public securities and can lose their entire investment.
