What Happens at a Final Map: The Last Step Before Selling Small-Lot Homes

Oliver Thornton · October 24, 2026

Approval of an SB 684 subdivision is a milestone, not the finish line. The approval creates a tentative map. The lots don’t legally exist until a final map records, and individual homes generally can’t be sold until they do. Here is what happens in between.

Tentative Map vs. Final Map

Tentative mapFinal map
What it isThe approved subdivision plan, with conditionsThe recorded, surveyed map that legally creates the lots
Who actsPlanning approvesSurveyor prepares; city engineer checks; county records
ResultPermission to proceedNew legal parcels, then new parcel numbers

What Happens Between the Two

  • Satisfying conditions. The tentative approval comes with conditions, such as utility connections, easements, fees or street improvements.
  • Survey and map preparation. A licensed surveyor prepares the final map to the city’s technical standards.
  • Map check. The city engineer or surveyor reviews it, often through a round or two of corrections.
  • Guarantees. Where improvements aren’t finished, the city may require an agreement or bond to secure them.
  • Recording. Once approved, the map records with the county, and the lots legally exist.

New Parcel Numbers

After recording, the county assessor assigns each new lot its own assessor’s parcel number. That step can take weeks to months. It matters because a separate APN is what lets each home be taxed, financed and sold on its own, which is the core advantage of a fee-simple small-lot subdivision. See how fee-simple small-lot homes are priced.

Why Timing Matters

The final map is often on the critical path to sales. If it records late, finished homes can sit waiting while interest accrues on the construction loan. Experienced developers work the final map in parallel with construction, so the lots exist by the time the homes are ready. Where the map falls in the schedule depends on the city’s conditions; see the Los Angeles entitlement process, step by step.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the difference between a tentative map and a final map?
A tentative map is the approved plan for a subdivision, with conditions. A final map is the recorded, surveyed document that legally creates the new lots once those conditions are met.

When can individual small-lot homes be sold?
Generally only after the final map records and the new lots legally exist, so each home can be conveyed on its own parcel.

Who assigns the new parcel numbers?
The county assessor assigns new assessor's parcel numbers (APNs) after the final map records, which can take some time.

Can construction start before the final map records?
It depends on the jurisdiction and project. Some projects build under permits issued before the final map records; others record first. Timing is set by the city's conditions.
